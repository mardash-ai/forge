Operating accounts & test tenants

The console's Data section — what it can do to a real account, what it deliberately cannot, and why the two credentials behind it are not interchangeable.

Where the data comes from

An account is not one thing. Half of it belongs to the platform — the login identity, the billing subscription, the household graph, the connector grants — and forge owns all of that. The other half belongs to the app: the rows in its own schema, keyed by owner id, that forge does not and must not know about.

The console reaches an app's half over the app's own HTTP surface, never its database. That is slower than a query and it is the point: the app keeps its invariants — the transactional local delete, the tombstone that signs a deleted user out, the two-marker test-tenant guard — and the console never becomes a second, unaudited way to mutate a datastore. “Nothing is done manually against a datastore” is a property of this arrangement, not a rule people follow.

Two credentials, two surfaces, no fallback

CredentialReachesCan it touch a real account?
CONSOLE_DORINDA_ADMIN_TOKEN/api/admin/*yes — it can erase one
CONSOLE_DORINDA_TEST_TOKEN/api/test/tenant/*no — structurally cannot

There is deliberately no “use whichever is set” path between them. A fallback is precisely how a reset would one day arrive at the purge surface. An unconfigured credential makes that screen report itself as unconfigured; it never silently borrows the other.

Production holds only the admin token. The test-control surface is dark there — its kill switch is off, so those routes return 404 — and the Test tenants screen reports “not configured”, which is the honest state of that environment rather than a broken screen.

Deleting an account

Two halves, in this order: the app deletes its own owner-scoped tables in one transaction, then one platform call tears down all ten platform subsystems — connectors revoked at the provider, MCP grants, billing, push, notifications, search, blobs, app events, memberships, and the login identity last.

  • You must type the account's email. Checked by the console against the row you were looking at and by the app against the identity forge holds. The realistic mistake is never “I didn't mean to delete an account” — it is “I didn't mean to delete that one”, and a yes/no dialog does not catch it.
  • A reason is required and lands in the audit row. “Someone with the operator password did it at 03:12” is not an answer anyone can act on later.
  • An incomplete erasure is never shown as success. The cascade is partial-tolerant, so it reports retained[] — anything it could not remove, with the reason. An empty retained is the only state that renders as done. The cascade is idempotent; retry by owner id, since the local rows (and the email→owner mapping) are already gone.

Test tenants

A tenant qualifies only with both the test_tenant flag and an @dorinda.test address. Neither is settable through any API — an endpoint that could set the flag would let a caller nominate a real account as a fixture and then erase it, collapsing every layer into one call.

ActionWhat it does
ResetEmpties the tenant and its household but keeps the account, its login, its entitlement and its connected AI. Preserving the connector is what makes a nightly suite possible — deleting it would mean a human re-authorising in a browser every run.
ClockMoves the tenant's virtual time and then settles: every firing path runs at the new time until nothing more happens. Firing cascades, so a single pass would leave a state no real elapsed time produces.
The sweeps are owner-scoped, down in the SQL. The production firing paths claim app-wide; running one at a fake future time without a scope would fire every real customer's pending reminders and calendar alerts early — real notifications, to real people, because a test moved a clock.

If the clock cannot reach a fixed point it reports settled: false rather than failing. That is a finding about the product — most likely a firing path re-arming itself at the same instant — and the operator should read it as data.

Everything here is audited

Every mutation is registered in the console's audited write table, and a test fails the build if a mutating route is not. The audit row is written before the attempt and updated after, so a crash mid-write still leaves a record — those are the rows you want at 3am. See Audit.
